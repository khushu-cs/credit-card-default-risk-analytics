💡 What I'd recommend

  1. Watch balance growth. Balances grew 32% in six months while credit lines stayed the same, so utilization climbed from 23% to 31%. That's a portfolio taking on more risk.
  2. Focus collections on customers already behind. They're 32% of customers but hold nearly two-thirds of the money that ended up in default.

💡 What I'd recommend

  1. Size of credit line is the clearest profile signal. Customers with lines of NT$50K or less default about 32% of the time, compared with 12% above NT$400K.
  2. For large credit lines, watch utilization. Customers with lines of NT$200K+ who use 90%+ of them default about 25% of the time, compared with about 14% for those using under 30%.
  3. Don't build decisions on demographics. Age, education and marital status differences are small, and fair-lending rules restrict using them.

💡 What I'd recommend

  1. Act on the first missed payment. One month late raises default risk from about 13% to 34%; two months late raises it to 69%.
  2. Track repeat lateness. Customers late in 4 or more of the last 6 months default 64% of the time. They're the priority for hardship plans or credit-line reductions.

About the dataset

Source: Yeh, I. (2009). Default of Credit Card Clients [Dataset]. UCI Machine Learning Repository. doi.org/10.24432/C55S3H. Licensed CC BY 4.0.

What it is: 30,000 real card holders of a bank in Taiwan. For each: credit limit, demographics, six monthly bills, six monthly payments, six monthly repayment-status codes (April–September 2005), and whether they defaulted the following month.

Currency: New Taiwan dollars (NT$). In 2005, US$1 was roughly NT$32.

Context: Taiwan went through a well-known card debt crisis in 2005–2006, which helps explain the very high 22% default rate. A typical US card portfolio defaults far less, so the patterns matter more than the levels.

Limitations to keep in mind

Six months only: no long-term trends, seasonality or account age.

No purchase data: no merchant categories, transactions or fraud. Banks don't publish these for privacy reasons.

Undocumented codes: repayment status -2 and 0 aren't defined by the source. I used the most common interpretation (no balance and revolving).

"Inactive but defaulted": 866 customers had no bills in all six months, yet 37% are marked as defaulting. That can't be explained from this data alone; in a real job I'd ask the data owner.
