What I'd recommend

  1. Treat rising utilization as an early warning. Balances grew 32% in six months while credit lines stayed the same, so utilization climbed from 23% to 31%.
  2. Start collections with customers already behind. They're a third of customers but hold nearly two-thirds of the money that went into default.

What I'd recommend

  1. Use credit-line size as the main profile signal. Customers with lines of NT$50K or less default about 32% of the time, compared with 12% above NT$400K.
  2. For large credit lines, watch utilization. Customers with NT$200K+ lines who use 90%+ of them default about 25% of the time, compared with about 14% for those using under 30%.
  3. Keep demographics out of decisions. Age, education and marital status differences are small, and fair-lending rules restrict using them.

What I'd recommend

  1. Act on the first missed payment. One month late raises default risk from about 13% to 34%; two months late raises it to 69%.
  2. Prioritise repeat lateness. Customers late in four or more of the last six months default 64% of the time. They're the first candidates for hardship plans or credit-line reductions.

What I'd recommend

  1. Point collections at the top of the ladder. The riskiest 20% of customers include about half of all defaulters, so effort there is about 2.5 times as efficient as random outreach.
  2. Reward the Low tier. About 17% of customers have under a 10% predicted chance of default. They're safe candidates for credit-line increases and retention offers.

About the dataset

Source: Yeh, I. (2009). Default of Credit Card Clients [Dataset]. UCI Machine Learning Repository. doi.org/10.24432/C55S3H. Licensed CC BY 4.0.

What it is: 30,000 real card holders of a bank in Taiwan. For each one: credit limit, demographics, six monthly bills, six monthly payments and six repayment-status codes (April–September 2005), and whether they defaulted the following month.

Currency: New Taiwan dollars (NT$). In 2005, US$1 was roughly NT$32.

Context: the data comes from Taiwan's 2005–06 card-debt crisis, which explains the very high 22% default rate. The patterns transfer to other portfolios; the levels don't.

Limitations

Six months only: no long-term trends, seasonality or account age.

No purchase data: no merchants, transactions or fraud. Banks don't publish these for privacy reasons.

Undocumented codes: repayment status -2 and 0 aren't defined by the source, so I used the common interpretation: no balance, and revolving.

Inactive but defaulted: 866 customers had no bills in six months, yet 37% are marked as defaulting. In a real job I'd raise this with the data owner.

Data: UCI Machine Learning Repository, Default of Credit Card Clients (Yeh, 2009), real customers, CC BY 4.0. Built with SQL (DuckDB), Python (Pandas, scikit-learn) and Plotly. Every number on this page comes from the code in the GitHub repository.
